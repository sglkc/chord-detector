\title[Evaluating CNN vs. SVM for Piano Chord Recognition Under Acoustic Domain Shift]{Evaluating CNN vs. SVM for Piano Chord Recognition Under Acoustic Domain Shift}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Cikal} \sur{Gemintang Seya}}\email{cikalgemintangseya1@gmail.com}

\author[1]{\fnm{Jasman} \sur{Pardede}}\email{jasman@itenas.ac.id}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Informatics}, \orgname{National Institute of Technology}, \orgaddress{\street{Neglasari}, \city{Bandung}, \postcode{40124}, \state{West Java}, \country{Indonesia}}}

\abstract{Audio-based chord recognition is fundamental to music information retrieval (MIR), yet classifier generalization under real-world distribution changes remains a critical vulnerability. This paper investigates Constant-Q Transform (CQT) features paired with either a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) or a tuned RBF Support Vector Machine (SVM) on a 36-class piano chord task, covering 12 chromatic root notes and 3 chord qualities. We deliberately isolate a simultaneous, three-factor acoustic domain shift encompassing changes in instrument timbre, recording device, and environmental noise. While in-domain cross-validation yields a perfect 1.00 accuracy for both architectures, the performance sharply diverges under cross-domain evaluation. The CNN maintains robust generalizability with 0.89 accuracy and 0.87 macro F1-score, whereas the SVM collapses to 0.29 accuracy and a 0.36 F1-score. Misclassification analysis indicates that the CNN's failures are confined to harmonically proximate chord pairs. Conversely, the SVM undergoes a global attractor collapse to C diminished, driven by the extreme sensitivity of global RBF kernel distances to spectral envelope shifts. These results demonstrate that the CNN's spatially local hierarchical feature learning yields superior structural resilience against real-world covariate shifts compared to global kernel methods, providing key architectural insights for robust and reliable audio classifiers.}

\keywords{Constant-Q Transform, Convolutional Neural Network, Support Vector Machine, generalization, domain shift}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

Recognizing musical chords directly from raw audio is a foundational task in music information retrieval (MIR), underpinning applications such as automatic music transcription, interactive music education tools, and real-time performance feedback systems \cite{Telila2023}\cite{Saputra2021}\cite{Moysis2023}. Unlike symbolic or MIDI-based chord recognition, audio-based recognition must contend with the acoustic realities of sound production: a chord played on one instrument, in one room, captured by one microphone, does not produce the same waveform as the identical chord played on a different instrument, in a different room, captured by a different microphone. This variability is not a peripheral nuisance but the central obstacle to deploying chord recognition systems in real-world settings, where the recording conditions encountered at inference time are rarely identical to those under which a model was trained \cite{Kusaka2024}.

This mismatch is termed acoustic domain shift and is primarily driven by principal factors. First, instrument variation where differing voice synthesis hardware alter timbre and displace chord vectors in the feature space \cite{Kusaka2024}\cite{Majchrzak2025}. Second, environmental noise which introduces room acoustics and ambient spectral energy that are absent during training \cite{Majchrzak2025}. Lastly, recording device variation where microphone frequency responses introduce hardware-induced spectral distortions \cite{Kusaka2024}. Critically, classification errors under domain shift are not merely a symptom of insufficient training data volume, they arise because the three factors above systematically change the acoustic feature distribution of the test data away from the distribution that the classifier was optimized on, regardless of how much training data was used within the original domain \cite{Kusaka2024}\cite{Majchrzak2025}.

To study this problem, a feature representation and classifier pairing must first be established for the in-domain case. Time-frequency representations that convert audio into image-like matrices, subsequently classified with convolutional architectures, are now the dominant paradigm across audio recognition tasks including environmental sound classification \cite{Wolf-Monheim2025}, acoustic scene classification, speech recognition \cite{Zaman2023}, and music analysis \cite{Moysis2023}. Among these representations, the Constant-Q Transform (CQT) occupies a distinctive position for keyboard and chord audio specifically. Unlike the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), which uses linearly spaced frequency bins, the CQT uses logarithmically spaced bins with its spacing governed by a constant quality factor, aligning each bin directly with a musical semitone on the chromatic scale \cite{Lacoste2007}. This alignment means that harmonic interval patterns appear as consistent geometric shapes in the CQT spectrogram, whereas the same intervals appear at different absolute bin spacings under STFT \cite{Li2021}.

Li \cite{Li2021} demonstrated this advantage empirically, reporting 84.2\% accuracy for CQT-based chord recognition versus 75.3\% for STFT and 80.8\% for CENS chromagrams under otherwise identical CNN architectures. Telila et al.\ \cite{Telila2023} further confirmed that CQT-derived features capture piano note frequency structure with 88.2\% frame-level transcription accuracy, and O'Hanlon and Sandler \cite{OHanlon2021} exploited this same musical-scale alignment to design FifthNet, a parameter-efficient chord recognition architecture. Together, this body of work establishes CQT not merely as one viable feature among many, but as a representation whose geometric structure is specifically matched to the harmonic organization of keyboard music, making it the appropriate feature choice for this study.

Understanding which combinations of spectral features and classifier architectures remain robust under acoustic domain shift is of substantial practical and theoretical importance. The piano chord classification problem provides a controlled vocabulary for such a study. With 36 classes formed by 12 chromatic root notes and 3 chord qualities (major, minor, diminished), the task requires fine-grained spectral discrimination: major and minor triads differ by a single semitone shift in the third, and diminished triads differ by a further semitone shift in the fifth, corresponding to shifts of one or two CQT frequency bins at the chosen resolution \cite{Harte2010}. This makes the task appropriate for controlled generalization analysis.

The two classifiers evaluated here represent distinct strategies. CNN learns spatially localized hierarchical feature detectors whose convolutional kernels respond to small time-frequency neighborhoods \cite{OShea2015}\cite{Purwono2022}. RBF-SVM learns a maximum-margin hyperplane defined by kernel similarity to training support vectors, where the RBF kernel measures global Euclidean distance between flattened feature vectors \cite{Wainer2020}. Under acoustic domain shift these strategies are expected to diverge, the CNN's locally defined representations should partially survive global spectral shifts, whereas the SVM's globally defined kernel distances may be severely distorted by the same shifts \cite{Zaman2023}.

Support Vector Machines with RBF kernels are an established baseline for musical chord classification tasks \cite{Cui2022} \cite{Putra2025}. Cui \cite{Cui2022} applied SVM with Pitch Class Profile features for chord recognition, while Putra and Sanjaya \cite{Putra2025} employed SVM with PCP features for automatic chord recognition on guitar and piano, both achieving high accuracy under within-distribution evaluation. However, neither study addressed cross-dataset or cross-instrument generalisation, which is the critical gap that the present work directly investigates.

The existing literature reveals a critical systemic gap: contemporary MIR research heavily prioritizes expanding chord vocabulary size \cite{Majchrzak2025} or introducing increasingly complex architectures, such as conformer-based models \cite{Akram2025} or transformer-attention mechanisms \cite{Kusaka2024} \cite{Rowe2021}. However, these systems are almost universally validated on in-domain benchmarks, leaving their structural generalizability unprobed. While a few recent studies have acknowledged the existence of an out-of-domain accuracy gap, they treat it purely as a data-scarcity issue to be bypassed via synthetic data augmentation \cite{Kusaka2024} \cite{Byambatsogt2020} or artificial generation strategies \cite{Majchrzak2025}, rather than diagnosing the underlying structural vulnerabilities of the chosen classifiers.

To date, the literature lacks a systematic, controlled evaluation that isolates how fundamentally distinct classification strategies behave under an unaugmented, three-factor acoustic domain shift. Rather than chasing incremental accuracy gains on static benchmarks, this work establishes a diagnostic baseline designed to uncover the structural root causes of classifier resilience and collapse under real-world covariate shifts.

We investigate these vulnerabilities using a strictly controlled experimental framework. The training and evaluation datasets are captured using completely separate keyboard instruments, audio synthesis engines, recording devices, and physical acoustic environments, while holding all other musical and variables entirely constant. The paper's specific contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[1.]

\item An acquisition of two independently recorded piano chord datasets, deliberately varied along the three principal acoustic domain-shift factors, instrument, recording environment, and recording device, to enable a controlled study of cross-domain generalization in piano chord recognition.

\item An extraction of Constant-Q Transform (CQT) spectrogram features from the recorded audio, selected for their alignment with the musical chromatic scale, to represent chord harmonic structure in a form suitable for both convolutional and kernel-based classifiers.

\item An evaluation and comparison of a CNN and an RBF-SVM classifier, both trained under identical in-domain conditions, to examine how each classifier's strategy is affected when the acoustic domain shifts between the training and evaluation datasets.

\end{enumerate}

Scenario 1 establishes an upper bound on classifier performance when instrument, environment, and recording device are held constant between training and testing, isolating whether the CQT feature space is discriminative at all for this 36-class problem. Scenario 2 then changes all three acoustic domain-shift factors simultaneously and evaluates both classifiers without any fine-tuning, domain adaptation, or exposure to the new domain during training, directly measuring the classifiers' structural robustness to real-world deployment conditions. The two scenarios are evaluated using the same trained model instances, no model is retrained between Scenario 1 and Scenario 2.

\subsection{Dataset acquisition}\label{subsec3_1}

Audio recordings are collected using a custom web-based MIDI recording application that connects to a keyboard via USB MIDI, programmatically triggers chords by sending simultaneous MIDI note-on events, and captures the resulting audio through a connected microphone (Online Resource~1). This deterministic triggering approach eliminates manual playing errors while retaining organic acoustic properties arising from the instrument's physical resonance and synthesis.

The chord vocabulary covers 36 classes formed by the combination of 12 chromatic root notes (C, C$\sharp$, D, D$\sharp$, E, F, F$\sharp$, G, G$\sharp$, A, A$\sharp$, B) and 3 chord quality types (major, minor, diminished). All chords are voiced in the fourth octave, which provides optimal spectral coverage within the 6-octave CQT range and clear harmonic separability between qualities \cite{Telila2023} \cite{Harte2010}. The inclusion of the diminished quality tests classifier sensitivity to a harmonically dense chord type in which all intervals are minor thirds, creating greater inter-class harmonic proximity than major or minor chords \cite{Byambatsogt2020}.

Two independent datasets are recorded to enable cross-domain evaluation:

\begin{enumerate}[1.]

\item \textbf{Training dataset}: 200 samples per class, 7,200 total. Recorded using a Xiaomi Redmi Pad 2 microphone from a Techno T-9890i keyboard with Acoustic Grand Piano voice. Note attack delays are randomized in the range 0--150\,ms to approximate the natural performance of human keystroke timing rather than perfectly simultaneous MIDI note-on events \cite{Bella2011} and prevent overfitting to perfect note onsets.

\item \textbf{Evaluation dataset}: 40 samples per class, 1,440 total. Recorded using the internal microphone of an ASUS ROG Flow X13 laptop from a Yamaha PSR-S910 keyboard with Grand Piano voice, in a different physical room. Note attack delays are randomized in the range 0--100\,ms. Half of the samples per class are recorded at a relatively higher input volume level to introduce additional inter-sample acoustic variation.

\end{enumerate}

The two datasets differ systematically in instrument brand, piano voice synthesis, recording device, and room acoustics. No samples from the evaluation dataset were used at any stage of training, validation, or hyperparameter optimization. This strict separation ensures that cross-domain results measure genuine cross-domain generalization. All recordings are stored in OGG/OPUS format at 48,000\,Hz sample rate, 32-bit depth, mono channel, without post-processing filters. The distribution of audio durations across the training dataset is provided in Online Resource~2.

\subsection{CQT feature extraction}\label{subsec3_2}

Feature extraction uses the Constant-Q Transform via \texttt{librosa.cqt()}. The CQT computes a time-frequency representation in which each frequency bin $f_k$ satisfies a constant quality factor:

\begin{equation}
Q = \frac{f_k}{f_{k+1} - f_k} = \frac{1}{2^{1/B} - 1}
\label{eq:cqt}
\end{equation}

where $B$ is the number of bins per octave \cite{Li2021}\cite{Lacoste2007}. This logarithmic spacing aligns each bin with a musical semitone, making the spectrogram invariant to chord transposition and producing geometrically consistent harmonic patterns regardless of root pitch.

The extraction parameters are listed in Table~\ref{tab:cqt-extraction-params}. The configuration of 36 bins per octave (3 bins per semitone) provides frequency resolution sufficient to resolve the interval differences between major ($+4, +3$ semitones), minor ($+3, +4$ semitones), and diminished ($+3, +3$ semitones) triads \cite{Akram2025}\cite{OHanlon2021}. Each sample is truncated to a fixed duration of 2.0\,s, using Equation~\ref{eq:frames} that yields a fixed frame length of $188$ frames and a uniform feature shape of $216 \times 188 \times 1$.

\begin{equation}
\text{frames} = \frac{\text{duration} \times \text{sample rate}}{\text{hop size}}
\label{eq:frames}
\end{equation}

\subsection{CNN architecture and training}\label{subsec3_3}

The CNN architecture is adapted from Wolf-Monheim \cite{Wolf-Monheim2025}, originally designed for environmental sound classification on ESC-50. Its compact four-block convolutional structure without recurrent layers or attention mechanisms is appropriate for fixed-window classification from static spectrograms in this study.

Table~\ref{tab:cnn-architecture} specifies the complete layer sequence. Input batch normalization ensures consistent CQT magnitude statistics across samples and recording conditions, which is particularly important for cross-domain robustness since different recording environments may produce different absolute magnitude ranges. The four Conv2D-MaxPooling2D blocks build a progressive feature hierarchy: the first block (64 filters) captures low-level spectral texture; the second (128 filters) encodes mid-level harmonic interval patterns; the third and fourth (256 filters each) represent high-level chord quality abstractions. The $3\times3$ receptive field matches the 1--2 bin frequency shifts distinguishing chord types, while MaxPooling provides spatial subsampling and limited translation invariance \cite{Purwono2022}. Dropout (rate 0.5) prevents co-adaptation and serves as an implicit regularizer against overfitting to training instrument timbre.

\subsection{SVM hyperparameter tuning}\label{subsec3_4}

An RBF-kernel SVM is configured using scikit-learn's \texttt{SVC}. Each $216 \times 188$ CQT matrix is flattened to a 40,608-dimensional vector prior to classification. Hyperparameter tuning uses Optuna with a \texttt{RandomSampler} over 10 trials with the search space $C \in \{0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000\}$ and $\gamma \in \{10^{-6}, 10^{-5}, 10^{-4}, 10^{-3}\}$ following Wainer and Fonseca \cite{Wainer2020}. Each trial is evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation accuracy on the training dataset.

\subsection{5-fold cross-validation}\label{subsec3_5}

Cross-validation across 5 folds is performed on the full 7,200-sample training dataset to assess the robustness of in-domain performance independently of a single train/test split. In each fold, 80\% is allocated to training and 20\% to testing. Both classifiers are fully retrained on each fold. For the CNN, each fold involves complete re-initialization and retraining from scratch with a fixed per-fold random seed. For the SVM, each fold likewise involves complete retraining of a new RBF-SVM using the fixed Optuna-selected hyperparameters, with the feature scaler refit exclusively on that fold's training partition to prevent leakage across folds.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}\label{subsec3_6}

Classification performance is evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score derived from the confusion matrix. For a binary partition of one class against all others, the four fundamental quantities are True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP), and False Negatives (FN) \cite{Hossin2015}.

\subsection{In-domain cross-validation performance}\label{subsec4_2}

Under Scenario 1, both the CNN and the RBF-SVM achieve perfect classification performance. Across all five folds of the cross-validation, both models uniformly yield an accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score of 1.00. This absolute performance demonstrates that when the acoustic environment, instrument, and recording equipment remain stable between the training and testing partitions, the CQT feature space provides completely distinct and separable patterns for all 36 piano chord classes.

\subsection{Cross-domain evaluation performance}\label{subsec4_3}

To evaluate structural robustness against acoustic domain shift, the trained models are subjected to Scenario 2 using the held-out 1,440-sample evaluation dataset without any architectural fine-tuning, weight updates, or exposure to the target domain during the optimization phase. Under these cross-domain conditions, the classification performance of the two architectures sharply diverges.

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:model-comparison}, the CNN demonstrates robust generalization capabilities, maintaining a cross-domain accuracy of 0.89 and a macro F1-score of 0.87. Conversely, the RBF-SVM experiences a severe performance collapse, dropping precipitously to a classification accuracy of 0.29 and a macro F1-score of 0.36.

In contrast, the per-class breakdown reveals that the RBF-SVM's drop to a 0.29 accuracy baseline is not caused by generalized, random errors. This diagnostic profile indicates a global single-class attractor collapse, where the system defaults to predicting a single chord quality regardless of the true audio input. This phenomenon highlights the extreme vulnerability of global kernel distance boundaries to unaugmented covariate shifts and motivates the deeper confusion matrix diagnostic analysis provided in the following section.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec5}

\subsection{CNN error structure under domain shift}\label{subsec5_1}

To understand why the CNN's cross-domain accuracy of 0.89 falls short of its in-domain 1.00, we examine its confusion matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn-eval-confusion}) directly, using it as a diagnostic tool rather than a summary statistic.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{Fig3.eps}
\caption{CNN confusion matrix on the cross-domain evaluation dataset (1,440 samples, 40 per class)}
\label{fig:cnn-eval-confusion}
\end{figure}

Misclassifications are not randomly distributed but concentrated in harmonically proximate chord pairs. The largest single error (A\_diminished $\to$ A$\sharp$\_major, 20 counts) involves a quality shift combined with a semitone root displacement, consistent with a global downward frequency shift of the evaluation instrument's spectral envelope moving the CQT pattern of A\_diminished into the neighborhood of A$\sharp$\_major.

The next largest cluster (C\_diminished $\to$ C$\sharp$\_diminished, 16 counts) is a pure semitone root shift with preserved quality. This confusion matrix therefore provides direct visual evidence that the CNN's residual errors are boundary-case failures at single-semitone class margins, not arbitrary or catastrophic misclassifications, supporting the interpretation that spatially local convolutional features partially, but not perfectly, survive the acoustic domain shift.

\subsection{SVM attractor collapse}\label{subsec5_2}

The SVM's confusion matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:svm-eval-confusion}) reveals a fundamentally different, and more severe, misclassification.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{Fig4.eps}
\caption{SVM confusion matrix on the cross-domain evaluation dataset (1,440 samples)}
\label{fig:svm-eval-confusion}
\end{figure}

The matrix shows the model overwhelmingly defaulting to C\_diminished\_4 regardless of true label. This pattern is directly attributable to the mechanics of the RBF kernel: when the Yamaha PSR-S910's different piano voice synthesis produces CQT vectors with a globally shifted spectral envelope, the Euclidean distances between test vectors and all training support vectors are systematically altered. The support vectors of C\_diminished\_4 happen to occupy the position closest to the new distribution's center of mass, producing the observed attractor.

Confusion matrix inspection thus directly visualizes why the SVM's error is structural and global, arising from the flattened 40,608-dimensional feature representation treating a global spectral shift identically everywhere, rather than a series of independent local misclassifications as seen in the CNN.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec6}

This paper evaluated the generalizability of audio-based piano chord recognition systems under a simultaneous acoustic domain shift. By comparing a CNN against a hyperparameter-tuned RBF-SVM using CQT features, this study demonstrated that while both approaches achieve ideal classification under stable in-domain conditions, their robustness diverges drastically when exposed to real-world acoustic shifts. While the CQT feature space proves to be fully discriminative under controlled environments, the CNN maintains high cross-domain generalizability under deployment conditions, whereas the RBF-SVM suffers a severe performance collapse.

Misclassification analysis confirms that the CNN out-of-domain errors are confined to harmonically proximate class boundaries. On the other hand, the SVM's reliance on global kernel distances leaves it highly vulnerable to acoustic shifts, resulting in a catastrophic global single-class attractor collapse. These insights provide vital architectural guidance for music information retrieval (MIR) applications, demonstrating that local feature tracking offers an inherent structural advantage over global distance methods when deploying audio classifiers in variable environments.

Because this study purposely avoided artificial data augmentation or noise filtering to isolate the raw baseline impact of acoustic shifts, addressing the remaining performance gap remains an important objective. Future research will focus on closing the remaining accuracy gap via noise removal and domain adaptation. Furthermore, expanding the chord vocabulary past controlled fourth-octave triads and testing across diverse recording environments are essential steps toward building reliable real-world audio transcription tools.
